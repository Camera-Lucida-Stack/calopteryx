Written 8 October 2026 and agreed by the operator the same day, before any draft was written. Order agreed: nudging, defaults, choice overload. The operator chose consumer behaviour as the topic and this paper as one of three to take up, and confirmed on 8 October 2026 that she has no professional or personal connection with any of its authors.

## The work

Register the central claim of Jachimowicz, J. M., Duncan, S., Weber, E. U. and Johnson, E. J. (2019), "When and why defaults influence decisions: a meta-analysis of default effects", Behavioural Public Policy 3(2), 159-186, doi:10.1017/bpp.2018.43 (published online 24 January 2019; open access under CC BY 4.0; Crossref: no correction). A reproduction check may follow, scoped separately.

The claim, as the abstract states it: "we conduct a literature search and meta-analysis of the 58 default studies (pooled n = 73,675) that fit our criteria. While our analysis reveals a considerable influence of defaults (d = 0.68, 95% confidence interval = 0.53–0.83), we also discover substantial variation". The abstract adds: "we find that defaults in consumer domains are more effective and in environmental domains are less effective." The second finding is a separate claim and may be registered on its own, building on the first.

These quotations were taken from the abstract as Crossref holds it; every character is checked against the published PDF before anything is drafted.

## The charter's four questions

1. **Importance.** Defaults, the option that takes effect unless a person changes it, are the best-known tool of choice architecture and are used in pensions, organ donation, energy tariffs and online retail. This paper is the standard estimate of how strong they are (488 citations in OpenAlex on 8 October 2026), and its finding that defaults work best in consumer settings bears directly on marketing practice. Mertens et al. (2022, PNAS) report a similar figure for defaults (d = 0.62, 95% CI 0.52 to 0.73, across 128 effect sizes, in the corrected Table 2 of May 2022), but no re-analysis of these 58 effect sizes with a correction for publication bias has been found.
2. **Checkability.** Good. The paper states: "The meta-analysis data and code are publicly available via the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/tcbh7)." The project holds the effect-size data (defaultmetadata.csv: 58 rows, whose sample sizes sum to the paper's 73,675) and the R code for the model, a random-effects model fitted by restricted maximum likelihood with the metafor package. The project sets no licence, so, as with Pennycook, a receipt would fetch the files from OSF by fingerprint and never copy them. A reproduction would re-run the authors' model on their data in R and compare the result with d = 0.68 (0.53 to 0.83).
3. **Competence.** A random-effects meta-analysis is within Calopteryx's competence, and the operator can follow and judge a claim about whether defaults change choices, and a check that compares one figure with another.
4. **Sensitivity.** The data are summary statistics from published studies; no personal data are involved. Calopteryx knows of no relationship between the operator and the authors, and she confirmed none. Nothing on the record concerns defaults or consumer choice, so the work does not touch Dr Hulme's agents' work.

## The playbook's three further questions

1. **Stakes on the platform.** Nothing on the record concerns defaults or nudging (all claims searched on 8 October 2026), so no claim rests on this one yet.
2. **Is Calopteryx the right agent?** For registration, yes. For a later check, the data and code make a reproduction straightforward, and a re-analysis with a correction for publication bias would be a robustness test, worth doing as new work but never counted towards the claim's credence.
3. **What each outcome would add.** Registration puts the standard estimate of the default effect on the record with a test. The test would be written so that the claim is refuted if the authors' own model, re-run on their posted data, gives a pooled d outside a tolerance set from the paper's own figures and interval. A match would show the published figure follows from the published data; a mismatch would be checked with great care before filing.
