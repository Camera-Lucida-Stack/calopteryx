# Scoping note: registering Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd (2010) on choice overload

Written 8 October 2026, for the operator's agreement. The operator chose consumer behaviour as the topic and this paper as one of three to take up, and confirmed on 8 October 2026 that she has no professional or personal connection with any of its authors.

## The work

Register the central claim of Scheibehenne, B., Greifeneder, R. and Todd, P. M. (2010), "Can There Ever Be Too Many Options? A Meta-Analytic Review of Choice Overload", Journal of Consumer Research 37(3), 409-425, doi:10.1086/651235 (Crossref: no correction or retraction). A reproduction check may follow, scoped separately.

The claim, as the abstract states it: "In a meta-analysis of 63 conditions from 50 published and unpublished experiments (N = 5,036), we found a mean effect size of virtually zero but considerable variance between studies."

The results section gives the figures: "The mean effect size of choice overload across all 63 data points according to equation 1 is D = 0.02 (95% confidence interval [CI95] -0.09 to 0.12). The between-study variance is τ² = 0.12 (CI95 0.08 to 0.25)", with Q(62) = 192 (p < .001) and I² = 68% (p. 414). These quotations were taken from a text extraction of the publisher's PDF that garbled the minus and equals signs; every character is checked against the PDF itself before anything is drafted.

## The charter's four questions

1. **Importance.** Choice overload, the idea that more options can make people less likely to choose or less satisfied with what they choose, is among the best-known claims in consumer research and is widely taught and applied in retail and product design. This paper is the main evidence that the average effect is close to zero (1,076 citations in OpenAlex on 8 October 2026). It is directly disputed: Chernev, Böckenholt and Goodman's own meta-analysis (2015, Journal of Consumer Psychology 25(2), 333-358, doi:10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002) reports that once moderators are taken into account the effect of assortment size "is significant—a finding counter to the data reported by prior meta-analytic research". Both positions deserve a place on the record, and this one came first.
2. **Checkability.** Promising. Table 1 of the paper prints all 63 data points, each with its effect size, sampling variance, weight, set sizes, sample size and moderator codes, and the method is stated (a random-effects model, τ² by restricted maximum likelihood, in R). That should allow the pooled estimate, τ², Q and I² to be recomputed in open software. No posted dataset has been found, so the data would have to be transcribed from the printed table; the transcription would be done twice, independently, and compared, and would carry its own fingerprint. Seven data points were set to zero because their studies reported only "nonsignificant" results; the paper marks them, and the check would keep them as the authors did. Whether a transcribed table may be bundled, given the journal's copyright, is to be settled before any receipt; registration alone needs none of it.
3. **Competence.** A random-effects meta-analysis is within Calopteryx's competence. The operator can follow the claim, which is about whether an effect familiar from consumer life holds on average, and the outcome of a check would be a direct comparison with printed figures.
4. **Sensitivity.** The studies concern ordinary consumer choices by adult participants; no personal data are involved, since the paper reports only summary figures. Calopteryx knows of no relationship between the operator and the authors, and she confirmed none. Nothing on the record concerns choice overload, so the work does not touch Dr Hulme's agents' work.

## The playbook's three further questions

1. **Stakes on the platform.** Nothing on the record concerns choice overload or consumer choice (all claims searched on 8 October 2026), so no claim rests on this one yet.
2. **Is Calopteryx the right agent?** For registration, yes: the paper's figures and scope are clear, and registering it opens the topic on the record. A later check by Calopteryx would count towards the claim's credence but never towards the independent checks needed to establish or refute it.
3. **What each outcome would add.** Registration makes the claim visible and checkable. The test would be written so that the claim is refuted if a re-analysis of the authors' own 63 data points, by their stated method, gives a mean effect whose 95% interval excludes zero or differs materially from D = 0.02; the exact thresholds are set from the paper's own figures and stated uncertainty, as the playbook requires. A later registration of Chernev et al. (2015) could stand beside it, so that the record holds both sides of the dispute.
