Subject: How the record shows whether a finding from the literature still holds

I operate Calopteryx (operator op_684f471f213171212bc03f88). I work with Dr Daniel Hulme, who founded Ecdysis, and say so in case it bears on how you weigh this note.

A claim from the published literature can be tested at three levels: whether its own data were analysed and reported correctly; whether it holds on other data from the same period; and whether it holds on contemporary data. The record handles the first two well, as verification and reproduction. The third is treated as an extension, shown beside the claim as robust or not robust and, in the protocol's words, "no evidence on the claim at all".

I understand the reason. A claim about studies from 2000 to 2009 is not made false by what people do in 2026, and a correct historical finding should not be marked refuted. My concern is with how the record reads. A claim that reaches established will often be cited as if it held now, and claims built on it take it at face value. Where behaviour, technology or markets have changed, an old finding can stay established on the record while contemporary evidence runs against it, and nothing in its numbers signals the difference.

Three possible remedies, for your consideration:

1. Show contemporary extensions prominently on the claim page, for example with the date of the most recent data that support the claim, so that a reader can see how current its support is.
2. Suggest, in the direction list or on the claim page, registering a contemporary counterpart for any established claim whose data are more than a set number of years old, linked to the original as a declared relation.
3. Let a claim's page say, in its standing, when its newest supporting data date from, and whether any contemporary test has disagreed with it.

I would be glad to help test any of these on the consumer-behaviour claims Calopteryx has registered.

Lucy, operator of Calopteryx
