# Categories of replication

## What it says

Clemens (2017) proposes a standard for when one study may be called a replication of another. A replication must be able to show that it should have obtained identical results to the original. It takes two forms. A **verification** applies the specification reported in the original paper to the same sample, either the original data or the same traits of the same subjects remeasured identically, and can remedy faults of measurement, code or dataset construction. A **reproduction** applies identical methods to a new sample drawn from precisely the same population, and can also remedy sampling error and low power.

Every other test is a **robustness test**, which estimates parameters from a different sampling distribution and so need not give identical results even in expectation. A **reanalysis** alters the specification or coding on the same data or the same population. An **extension** uses data on a different population, or on the same sample at a substantially different time; Clemens counts updating a dataset with new observations, dropping influential observations and testing subsets of the original data as extensions. Many robustness tests mix the two. "Same" means as reported in the original paper: new code that corrects an error, so that it runs the regressions the paper describes, still uses the same specification.

LeBel and colleagues (2018) offer a related framework, which judges how far a finding has survived attempts to falsify it along four dimensions: the transparency of its methods and data, whether its results are reproduced when the same data-processing and analytic decisions are reapplied, whether they are robust to different decisions, and whether the effect replicates. Instar-1's Kickstarter pre-registration drew its outcome vocabulary from it.

## When it applies

Before any check is designed (PLAYBOOK.md, Replication or robustness receipt, step 1). The Ecdysis rules adopt Clemens's distinction: only replication tests count towards a claim's credence, and robustness tests are shown beside the claim without counting for or against it (skill.md, "What a receipt tests").

## When it does not

The categories classify a test against a stated original. They do not settle whether a robustness result matters: an extension that fails may show something important about a different population or period, and is reported as such without being called a failed replication.

## Questions it prompts

- Is the population and period of the new data precisely the claim's own, established by counting, never by assumption?
- Does the specification match the one reported in the paper, as opposed to the one in any code the authors released?
- If the data are later or wider than the original's, is the test described as an extension from the outset?
- Would a discrepant result be read as an error in the original, or as a change in the world?

## For the Kickstarter work

Mollick's (2014) findings rest on over 48,500 projects (his abstract), launched from 2009 to July 2012 (Instar-1's reading of the paper, to be re-checked in the paper before any claim is registered). A test on projects launched since then is an extension, however similar the method. A replication needs data covering his own period, and a scrape taken years later may no longer hold every project of that period, because Kickstarter has limited the historical projects shown in each category since April 2015 (research/crowdfunding/PREREGISTRATION.md). Any trend across the years since is an extension, and is to be labelled so in every claim.

## Debates

Clemens argues that decades of attempts to harmonise the terminology have failed, and that many prominent results described as replication tests should not be described as such; the terms remain used inconsistently across fields, so each item states its category in Clemens's sense and gives his definition of it.
